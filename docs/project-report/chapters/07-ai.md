# Artificial Intelligence and Predictive Maintenance

## 7.1 Introduction

This chapter describes the analytics of Industrial Twin-X: how training data is generated, how features are built, which models are used, how they are evaluated and what the results mean. Every number in this chapter comes from `research/reports/ai-evaluation/metrics.json`, produced by `research/experiments/train.py` on simulated data. **None of these results indicates performance on real industrial equipment.** The faults are generated by equations, so they are cleaner and more separable than real faults. The results should therefore be read as an upper bound on what these methods achieve in this simulation, not as an estimate of field performance.

The analytics address four tasks:

1. **Anomaly detection**: does a machine's recent behaviour differ from normal?
2. **Fault classification**: which known fault best explains the behaviour?
3. **Degradation estimation**: how worn is a bearing?
4. **Short-horizon risk**: how likely is a functional failure in the next 15 simulated minutes?

## 7.2 Dataset Generation

The generator (`research/datasets/generate.py`) runs the simulator headless, without a broker or database, for 80 runs of 3,600 simulated seconds: 20 runs each of four classes (normal, bearing wear, overheating and pump leak), with seeds 1000 to 1079. Each seed also draws the run's parameters:

- the target machine: any of the six rotating machines for bearing wear and overheating, either pump for leaks;
- the fault severity, uniform in [0.7, 1.3];
- the fault onset, uniform in [300, 900] seconds;
- the time of day at the start of the run, uniform over 24 hours, so that "normal" covers the full daily ambient cycle.

Edge protection is disabled during generation, so faults develop naturally instead of being cut short by the edge.

The first version of the generator started every run at the same time of day. Once noticed, this was corrected before the final training: normal behaviour would otherwise have been learned over only one hour of the ambient cycle, and long live runs would have raised false alarms as the simulated afternoon warmed the hall.

Labels come from hidden simulator state that is never published. A window on the target machine after onset is labelled with the fault only once the fault is *physically manifest*: wear has risen by at least 0.10 since onset, winding temperature has risen by at least 8 K since onset, or the leak fraction has reached 0.03. Windows after onset but before manifestation are labelled `pending` and excluded from precision and recall, because no detector can be expected to see a fault that has not yet changed the physics. Windows from a failed machine are labelled `failed` and excluded. All other windows are `normal`, including those from machines other than the target in a fault run.

The dataset contains 57,120 windows: 51,083 normal, 1,335 bearing wear, 1,556 overheating, 1,963 pump leak, 522 pending and 661 failed.

## 7.3 Data Preprocessing

Within each 120-second window, readings for each signal are grouped into 5-second bins, and each bin is reduced to the **median across the redundant sensors**. This step does two things. It aligns readings from devices that report at different offsets within the 5-second cycle, and it limits the influence of any single bad sensor. A unit test confirms that a sensor reporting 300 °C beside two sensors reporting 60 °C does not change the window's mean winding temperature. Readings from quarantined sensors are excluded before binning. A window is used only if at least 12 of its 24 possible speed bins contain data.

Speed, current, power and flow are normalised by the machine's rated values, so a 7.5 kW conveyor and a 22 kW pump produce comparable features. The anomaly detectors are also fitted separately for each machine kind (motor, conveyor, robot and pump), because their normal operating patterns differ: the robot's duty cycle, for example, gives it a much larger power variance than a motor.

## 7.4 Feature Engineering

Each window produces 31 features (`modules/anomaly_detection/features.py`):

- for each of nine signals (normalised speed, current, power and flow; suction flow; winding temperature; bearing temperature; vibration; and discharge pressure), the **mean**, the **standard deviation** and the **slope** per minute from a least-squares line, giving 27 features;
- four **derived ratios** that remove the effect of operating point:
  - vibration per unit speed, since vibration rises with speed even without wear;
  - leak ratio, 1 − delivered flow / suction flow, which is near zero for a healthy pump;
  - pressure per speed squared, since the affinity laws make pressure scale with speed squared;
  - power per unit speed.

Signals absent on a machine (flow on a motor, for example) are set to zero. For classification, the regressor and the risk model, a one-hot encoding of the machine kind is appended.

## 7.5 Anomaly Detection

Both detectors are trained on **normal windows only** from the training runs, and both are normalised so that a score of 1.0 sits exactly on the alarm threshold. The threshold for each machine kind is the 99.5th percentile of the training scores. A single window above 1.0 is not an alarm. The live pipeline raises an alarm only after two consecutive windows above 1.0, which is 60 simulated seconds at the 30-second hop, and it opens an incident only when the classifier also names a fault (Section 7.8).

## 7.6 Isolation Forest

The Isolation Forest builds random trees, each splitting on a randomly chosen feature at a random value. Anomalies are isolated in fewer splits, so a short average path length indicates an anomaly [@liu2008]. The implementation uses scikit-learn's `IsolationForest` with 100 trees and `contamination="auto"`, applied to standardised features for each machine kind. scikit-learn's `score_samples` returns lower values for more abnormal points [@sklearn_iforest], so the code negates it. It then rescales the result so that the median training score maps to 0 and the 99.5th percentile to 1.

## 7.7 Statistical Baseline

The baseline computes, for each machine kind, the mean μ and standard deviation σ of each of the nine *window-mean* features over normal training windows. A window's score is the largest absolute z-score across those nine features, divided by the 99.5th percentile of that maximum over the training windows (@eq:zscore):

$$ score = max_j | (x_j − μ_j) / σ_j |  /  q₀.₉₉₅ $$ {#eq:zscore}

This is a simple statistical detector. It was implemented as a baseline to show whether the Isolation Forest adds value. It is also the detector the live system uses, for the reasons given in Section 7.11.

## 7.8 Fault Classification

The fault classifier is a random forest [@breiman2001] with 100 trees, a minimum leaf size of 2 and balanced class weights. It predicts one of four classes (normal, bearing wear, overheating and pump leak) from the 31 features plus the machine-kind encoding. It is trained on labelled training windows, excluding pending ones. The class probability of the predicted class is used as the confidence, which the authorisation policy compares with a threshold of 0.8 (Section 8.11).

A rule baseline (`rule_diagnosis`) flags a pump leak if the leak ratio exceeds 0.05, overheating if the mean winding temperature exceeds 85 °C, and bearing wear if vibration per unit speed exceeds 2.2 mm/s. It serves as a comparison and as the fallback when no trained models are available.

## 7.9 Predictive Maintenance

**Degradation estimation.** A gradient-boosting regressor (150 trees, depth 3, learning rate 0.08) estimates hidden bearing wear from the features. It is trained on windows from normal and bearing-wear runs. Wear is a simulator quantity, so this task is possible only because the simulation knows the true wear; in a real plant there would be no such label.

**Short-horizon risk.** A binary random forest (60 trees, minimum leaf size 3, balanced) predicts whether the machine will reach a functional failure within 900 simulated seconds. Functional failure means a failed machine, or a pump whose leak fraction has reached 0.30. Its probabilities are recalibrated with isotonic regression [@niculescu2005; @sklearn_calib], using three cross-validation folds grouped by run so that no trajectory appears on both sides of a calibration fold.

**Recommendation.** The function `recommend` maps the risk score and degradation estimate to a priority. A risk of at least 0.6 or a degradation of at least 0.7 is *high*; a risk of at least 0.25 or a degradation of at least 0.4 is *medium*; anything lower is *low*. It then attaches an action specific to the fault. Live verification after the initial publication found a flaw here. Once a leak had passed the functional-failure threshold, the model correctly predicted no *future* failure within the horizon, so the risk score fell to zero and the page advised "continue routine monitoring" for a pump with a diagnosed leak. The function was corrected so that a currently diagnosed fault is always *high* priority with the fault-specific action, and a regression test was added. This correction is in the working tree but not yet committed. A live run on 16 November 2026 confirmed it: the derated motor with a diagnosed overheating fault was shown as high priority (@fig:screenmaint in Appendix H).

**What is not claimed.** The system does not predict remaining useful life. The risk output is called a *risk score*, not a probability of failure, because its calibration is incomplete (Section 7.11).

## 7.10 Model Training

`research/experiments/train.py` splits the dataset with `GroupShuffleSplit`, grouping by run ID with 30% of runs held out and a split seed of 7 [@sklearn_gss]. This yields 56 training runs (39,489 windows) and 24 test runs (16,970 windows). The script raises an error if any run appears on both sides. Grouping by run is essential: overlapping windows from one trajectory are strongly dependent, and a random window-level split would leak information from test trajectories into training [@kaufman2012; @roberts2017].

All models use fixed random seeds. The trained models are saved together in one bundle (`research/models/current/bundle.joblib`), with metadata recording the feature list, preprocessing, Python and scikit-learn versions, seed and split. The bundle is excluded from version control and is regenerated by the two commands in Appendix A. The evaluated bundle is version `20261102T063522Z`.

The Isolation Forest, the planned primary detector, was clearly worse. Its recall was 0.540 against 0.997 for the baseline; it detected bearing wear about 390 seconds later on average; and it **missed all four overheating runs**. Combining the two detectors by taking the larger score kept the baseline's recall, but nearly doubled the machines with sustained false alarms (21 against 11), with almost no gain in latency. The live system therefore uses the statistical baseline. The Isolation Forest and the hybrid remain implemented and evaluated for comparison. This decision is recorded as ADR-005 in the design decisions.

**Why the baseline performed better here.** The following explanation is consistent with the evidence but was not tested in isolation; for example, no feature-subset ablation was run. The simulated faults mainly shift the *mean* of one or two signals: winding temperature for overheating, vibration for bearing wear. The baseline examines exactly the window-mean features and alarms on the single largest deviation, so a large shift in one feature is enough. The Isolation Forest works on all 31 standardised features and chooses the split feature at random. In a 31-dimensional space, a shift confined to one feature affects only the splits that happen to use that feature, while the many noisy standard-deviation and slope features dilute it. Slow thermal drift is the hardest case for the forest, because each window differs only slightly from the windows before it. The result is specific to this feature set and this simulation, and it does not show that Isolation Forests are unsuitable for industrial monitoring in general.

The baseline still had 11 machines with a sustained false alarm in 125.9 machine-hours of held-out normal operation. A false alarm alone opens no incident in the live system, because the classifier must also name a fault, but it does mark the machine as anomalous on the dashboard.

### Fault classification

The random forest classified every labelled test window correctly: macro-F1 1.000 with no off-diagonal entries in its confusion matrix. The rule baseline reached macro-F1 0.927. It made no false fault calls on normal windows, but labelled 129 bearing-wear, 47 overheating and 14 leak windows as normal (@tab:confusion). A perfect score should not be read as strong evidence. The simulated faults affect distinct, noise-free combinations of signals by construction: a leak is the only fault that changes the leak ratio, and overheating is the only one that raises winding temperature without raising vibration. **This result reflects the clean, controlled fault classes of the simulation and is not evidence of comparable accuracy on real equipment**, where faults overlap, co-occur and vary.

### Degradation and risk

The degradation regressor achieved a mean absolute error of 0.0089 in hidden wear, against 0.0173 for a linear model on vibration per unit speed and 0.0387 for predicting the training mean. The risk model reached a ROC-AUC of 0.956 and a Brier score of 0.0119, against 0.0218 for always predicting the training prevalence.

Discrimination is not calibration, however. @tab:reliability and @fig:reliability show that the model is well calibrated at low risk but **under-predicts** in the middle range. Windows given a risk near 0.50 failed within the horizon 98% of the time. The isotonic calibration was fitted on folds of the training data, and with relatively few positive windows the middle bins were poorly estimated, a limitation that the calibration literature anticipates for small samples [@sklearn_calib]. The output is therefore presented as a risk score that ranks machines, not as a calibrated probability.

## 7.12 Limitations of AI Models

- **Synthetic data.** All training and test data comes from the simulation. The models learn the simulation's equations, including its simplifications, and would need retraining and revalidation on real data.
- **Known fault classes only.** The classifier can only name the three faults it was trained on. An unknown fault can trigger the detector, but the classifier will mislabel it or call it normal, and the latter opens no incident.
- **Operating-point sensitivity.** The detectors were trained on machines at full speed. A deliberately derated machine therefore looks anomalous. During live verification, a motor derated to 60% after overheating showed an anomaly score above 100 and a second, low-confidence overheating incident, which correctly waited for approval. Training with varied operating points, or conditioning the detector on the commanded speed, would address this.
- **Small evaluation set.** The held-out set had 24 runs, with only four to seven per fault. Latency estimates have wide spread (for example, SD 176 s for bearing wear), and a different split could change the numbers noticeably.
- **One split.** The models were evaluated on a single grouped split, not with repeated cross-validation, so no confidence interval is reported for the detection metrics.
- **Risk calibration.** The risk score under-predicts in the middle range, as shown above.
- **No remaining useful life.** Remaining useful life is not estimated, and the degradation estimate refers to a simulator variable that has no measured counterpart in a real plant.
